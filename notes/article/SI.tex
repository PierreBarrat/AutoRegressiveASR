\section{Autoregressive evolution model} % (fold)
\label{sec:autoregressive_evolution_model}

\subsection{Simplified expression for a homogeneous $\mathbf{H}$ }

For each site $i$, the main difference between our model and a traiditional GTR is that the equilibrium frequencies of the Markov chain are computed using the context at the previous sites $1,\ldots,i-1$. 
Considering \eqref{eq:Q_decomposition} and \eqref{eq:autoregressive_site_propagator}, this means that the diagonal matrix is determined using the generative model. 
On the other hand, the symetric matrix $\mathbf{H}$ can be given any value without changing the long term generative properties of the dynamical model, \emph{i.e.} \eqref{eq:autoregressive_long_term}. 
Here, we show that if the transitions defined by $\mathbf{H}$ are uniform, \emph{i.e.} $H_{ab} = \mu$ for any $a \neq b$, the propagator takes a simplified form: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	\label{eq:autoregressive_simplified_propagator}
	&q_i(b_i \vert a_i, b_{<i}, t) = e^{-\mu t} \delta_{a_i, b_i} + (1- e^{-\mu t})p_i(b_i \vert b_{<i}),\\
	&P(\mathbf{b} \vert \mathbf{a}, t) = \prod_{i=1}^L q_i(b_i \vert a_i, b_{<i}, t).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The interpretation of the site propagator $q_i(b_i \vert a_i, b_{<i}, t)$ is straightforward: if no mutation occurs with probability $e^{-\mu t}$, site $i$ remains in its original state $a_i$; otherwise, with probability $(1- e^{-\mu t})$, it is resampled using the equilibrium probability given by the generative model and the context of the sequence $p_i(b_i \vert b_{<i})$. 
Note that the assumption of a scalar matrix is reasonable if one wishes to ignore the different transition rates between amino-acids. 


% To prove that the expression above is correct, we use the simplifying assumption that $H_{ab} = 1$ for any $a \neq b$. 
% The generalization to an arbitrary $\mu$ is straightforward. 
To lighten notation, we drop the explicit dependence on the position $i$ and the sequence context $b_{<i}$ by defining $p_b = p_i(b_i \vert b_{<i})$.
We will then compute the $n$ eigenvectors and eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$, where $n = 21$ for the amino acids and gap symbol. 
First, note that for the continuous time Markov chain to be well defined, we need the rows of $\mathbf{Q}$ to sum to $0$. 
We thus have the following expression for the elements of $\mathbf{Q}$: 

$$
Q_{ab} = \mu \begin{cases}
			p_b, & \text{if $a \neq b$},\\
            -\sum_{c\neq a} p_c = p_a - 1, & \text{if $a = b$}.
		 \end{cases}
$$

This means that the vector $\vec{p} = (p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ is the left-eigenvector of $\mathbf{Q}$ associated to eigenvalue $0$:

\begin{align*}
	\left(\vec{p}^{\,T}\mathbf{Q}\,\right)_b &= \sum_a p_a Q_{ab}\\ 
	&= \mu\sum_{a\neq b} p_a p_b - p_b \mu \sum_{c\neq b} p_c = 0.
\end{align*}
Since the dynamics of the Markov chain are given by $\frac{\text{d}\vec{x}}{\text{d}t} = \vec{x}^{\,T} \mathbf{Q}$, this implies that $\vec{p}$ is an equilibrium state of the chain, as expected. 

We now prove that the other $n-1$ eigenvalues are equal to $-\mu$. 
Consider a vector $\vec{v}$ orthogonal to $\vec{p}$. 
The following calculation shows that $\vec{v}$ is a right-eigenvector of $\mathbf{Q}$ with eigenvalue $-\mu$: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	\left(\mathbf{Q} \vec{v}\,\right)_a &= \sum_b Q_{ab} v_b\\
	&= \mu\left( \sum_{b\neq a} p_b v_b - (p_a - 1)v_a \right)\\
	&= \mu\left( \langle\vec{p}, \vec{v}\rangle - v_a \right)\\
	&= -\mu v_a.
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The space of vectors orthogonal to $\vec{p}$ is of dimension $n-1$, therefore eigenvalue $-\mu$ is degenerate and the corresponding eigenvectors span a space of dimension $n-1$. 
We call $\vec{v}^{\,2}, \ldots, \vec{v}^{\,n}$ an orthogonal basis of this space. 
As the rows of $\mathbf{Q}$ sum to $0$, the remaining eigenvector is $\vec{v}^{\,1} = (1, \ldots, 1)$ and is associated to eigenvalue $0$. 
Similarly, we can also show that there exist $n-1$ left-eigenvectors associated to the eigenvalue $-\mu$, which note $\vec{u}^{\,2}, \ldots, \vec{u}^{\,n}$, defined by the relation $\langle\vec{u}^{\,a}, \vec{v}^{\,1}\rangle$ = 0. 
The first left-eigenvector is of course $\vec{u}^{\,1} = \vec{p}$. 

With the right choice of the basis $\vec{v}^{\,2}, \ldots, \vec{v}^{\,n}$ and $\vec{u}^{\,2}, \ldots, \vec{u}^{\,n}$, we have the relation $\langle{u}^a, \vec{v}^{\,a}\rangle = \delta_{ab}$. 
This means that we can write $\mathbf{Q}$ as

$$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{V\Lambda U},$$

where $\mathbf{V}$ has the right eigenvectors as columns, $\mathbf{U}$ the left eigenvectors as rows, and 

$$\mathbf{\Lambda} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & & & \\
& -\mu & & \\
& & \ddots & \\
& & & -\mu \\
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

We now compute the transition probability $q(b \vert a, t)$ by computing the exponential of $\mathbf{Q}$: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	q(b \vert a, t) &= \left(e^{\mathbf{Q}t}\right)_{ab}\\
	&= \left(\mathbf{V}e^{\Lambda t} \mathbf{U}\right)_{ab}\\
	&= \sum_{c=1}^n v_a^c e^{\Lambda_{cc}t}  u_b^c\\
	&= e^{-\mu t} \sum_c v_a^c u_b^c + (1-e^{-\mu t})v_a^1 u_b^1,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where we have used the fact that $\Lambda_{cc} = -\mu$ for $c>1$ and $0$ for $c=1$. 
Using the fact that $\mathbf{V}$ and $\mathbf{U}$ are each other's inverse, we have that $\sum_c v_a^c u_b^c = \delta_{ab}$. 
Furthermore, $v_a^1=1$ and $u_b^1 = p_b$
We thus obtain the desired result: 

\begin{equation}
	q(b \vert a, t) = e^{-\mu t} \delta_{ab} + (1-e^{-\mu t}) p_b.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Irreversibility of the autoregressive evolution model}

For each position $i$ and given a sequence context, the autoregressive model has the same structure as classical sequence evolution models. 
In particular, it is time reversible: given any two amino acid states $a_i$ and $b_i$, there is no objective way of determining whether  $a_i$ evolved in to  $b_i$ or the reverse. 
However, the propagator for full sequences \eqref{eq:autoregressive_propagator_full} is \emph{time irreversible}. 
We demonstrate this using a simple example. 

For a stochastic model, time reversibility is equivalent to respecting \emph{detailed balance}: for any two sequences  $\mathbf{a}$ and  $\mathbf{b}$ and any time $t$, one should have

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:detailed_balance_def}
	P(\mathbf{a})q(\mathbf{b}\vert \mathbf{a}, t) = P(\mathbf{b})q(\mathbf{a}\vert \mathbf{b}, t).
\end{equation}

We set out to design a simple autoregressive model that does not fulfill detail balance, showing that time reversibility is in general not fulfilled. 
Consider a sequence of length $L=2$ with binary states $a_1, a_2 \in \{0, 1\}$. 
Assume that the ``fitness landscape'' of this sequence is such that sequences $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are functional, while $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ are not. 
A corresponding sequence alignment would show the following distribution of sequences: 

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	\label{eq:toy_irreversible_eq}
	P(0,0) = P(1,1) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \varepsilon) \quad\text{and}\quad P(0,1) = P(1,0) = \frac{\varepsilon}{2},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon \ll 1$. 
A well trained autoregressive model would consequently have the following properties: 

\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
	\label{eq:toy_irreversible_autoregressive_eq}
		&p_1(0) = p_1(1) = \frac{1}{2},\\
		&p_2(0 \vert 0) = p_2(1 \vert 1) = 1-\varepsilon \quad\text{and}\quad p_2(0 \vert 1) = p_2(1 \vert 0) = \varepsilon,
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
which is the same distribution as in Eq.~\sref{eq:toy_irreversible_eq}. 

We now consider the two sequences $\mathbf{a} = (1, 1)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (0, 1)$ to show that the model does not respect detailled balance. 
Computing the two sides of Eq.~\sref{eq:detailed_balance_def} in this configuration, we find

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
	P(\mathbf{b})q(\mathbf{a}\vert \mathbf{b}, t) &\sim \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),\\
	P(\mathbf{a})q(\mathbf{b}\vert \mathbf{a}, t) &=  \frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\cdot(1-e^{-t})\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\left( e^{-t} + (1-e^{-t}) \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\\
	& \sim \frac{(1-\varepsilon)^2}{4}e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})\\ 
	&\sim \mathcal{O}(1).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The first expression is of order $\varepsilon$ since the probability that the process started in $\mathbf{b}$ is $P(\mathbf{b}) = \varepsilon/2$; even if $q(\mathbf{a}\vert \mathbf{b}, t)$ is a likely transition, the scenario $\mathbf{b} \rightarrow \mathbf{a}$ remains unlikely. 
For the second expression however, things are different. 
The right hand side is composed successively of $P(\mathbf{a}) = (1-\varepsilon)/2$, of $q_1(0 \vert 1, t) = (1-e^{-t})(1-\varepsilon)/2$, and of $q_2(1 \vert 1, b_{<2}=(0), t) = e^{-t} + (1-e^{-t}) \varepsilon/2$.
This shows that the scenario $\mathbf{a} \rightarrow \mathbf{b}$ is more likely, and we can assume that $\mathbf{a}$ is the ancestor of $\mathbf{b}$. 

Two things can now be discussed. 
First, even though the model does not satisfy detailed balance, it is straightforward to show that it satisfies \emph{global balance} and thus that it has a well defined equilibrium distribution. 
For instance, in the toy model above, the long term distribution of the propagator $q$ can be shown to be equal to the one in Eq.~\sref{eq:toy_irreversible_eq}. 
Secondly, the cause of irreversibility here is not epistasis in itself, but rather the structure of the autoregressive probability distribution. 
In our toy example, the model eagerly mutates the first residue without considering the second one, allowing a likely $\mathbf{a} \rightarrow \mathbf{b}$ transition. 
In fact, it is perfectly possible to design dynamical epistatic models that are time reversible \cite{alvarez_vivofunctionalphenotypes_2024}. 
Furthermore, irreversible models of evolution are not the most common but are still part of standard phylogenetic toolkits \cite{boussau_efficientlikelihoodcomputations_2006}.
